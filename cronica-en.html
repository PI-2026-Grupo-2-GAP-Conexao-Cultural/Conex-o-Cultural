The Taste of Prejudice

← Back

I will never forget the day when my simple lunch became the reason for my shame. Sometime ago, I was a very quiet student with no friends, and I liked staying in my own corner so that no one would bother me. It was just another ordinary day: I arrived at school at the same time, with the same enthusiasm for studying, and carrying the same lunchbox that accompanied me every day. The noise of students talking and laughing loudly bothered me a little, but I had to put up with it because I could not do anything about it.

The class went as usual—boring as always—and, finally, recess arrived and I could finally rest. I sat near some classmates with whom I had never spoken because there were no other empty places. My sacred moment of the day had begun: I opened my lunchbox and took out an acarajé prepared by my mother. That recipe was part of my family's history, a tradition of Afro-Brazilian origin that carried generations of culture and memory. To me, it was not just food; it was a small piece of my story.

I was quietly enjoying my lunch when my nightmare began: I became the center of attention! For some reason, my lunch looked strange to the other students, and I did not understand why. Some called it a mess that I had invented. Others complained about the smell. A few went even further and said that the food was disgusting. The laughter grew louder after every comment, while I tried to hide my lunchbox and wished I could disappear from there.

For a few moments, I thought about never taking that lunch to school again. Maybe it would be easier to eat something else, something that would not attract anyone's attention. However, when I looked at the acarajé that my mother had prepared with so much care, I remembered how many times she had told me about our family traditions. Why should I feel ashamed of something that was part of my history? After all, I had never made fun of anyone else's food.

I took a deep breath and, for the first time that day, decided to respond. I asked my classmates whether they really thought it was fair to judge an entire culture because of a dish they had never tried. I explained that acarajé has roots in African culture and an important history in Brazil, especially in Bahia. Some people kept laughing, but others remained silent, as if only then they realized that their jokes had gone too far. I was still nervous, but I no longer wanted to hide my food.

Then I made a proposal and asked whether anyone would like to try a piece. To my surprise, one of the students who had mocked my lunch the most accepted. He took a bite, chewed for a few seconds, and admitted that it tasted good. The others began asking questions about the ingredients and how my mother prepared it. For the first time, I felt that they were interested in listening to me. Excited, I began telling them more about the history of my lunch and its importance to my family. As I spoke, I realized that everyone was paying close attention—not to mock me, but to understand what I was trying to say.

After we talked about acarajé, other classmates began to share information about typical foods from their families. We discovered that each of us had different traditions and that these differences made our stories even more interesting. What had begun with ridicule and prejudice became a conversation filled with fascinating discoveries about new cultures.

In the following days, some classmates started talking to me more often. Not everyone became my friend immediately, but that experience changed the way I was seen at school. I no longer felt as much desire to hide what was part of my history.

For the first time, I did not feel excluded, but welcomed. Maybe that small gesture did not change everything overnight, but I discovered that sometimes a conversation can begin exactly where prejudice once existed.
